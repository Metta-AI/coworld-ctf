# DX Evidence: Onboarding Mechanics (Time-to-Hello-World, CLI Wizards vs Templates, In-Browser Editors)

Scope: general developer-experience research/industry evidence on onboarding mechanics — NOT bot-game platforms (covered elsewhere). Feeds a comparison of (a) fork-a-template-repo, (b) guided CLI wizard, (c) in-browser editor for a Paintbot-style game's "first door."

---

## 1. Time-to-hello-world studies/benchmarks

**Core finding: there is no rigorous, peer-reviewed, cross-industry benchmark study of "time to hello world" / "time to first API call" (TTFC). The concept is well-established in industry practice and repeatedly invoked, but the hard numbers that exist are almost all single-company, self-reported, before/after deltas — not controlled studies.**

- **Twilio onboarding redesign — 62% lift in first-message activation, 33% lift in 7-day production launches.** Twilio's own blog reports "62% improvement in first message activation" and "33% improvement in production launches within 7 days" for developers on a redesigned onboarding flow vs. the prior one. This is Twilio's own primary claim about its own product, not an external audit, but it is a named company reporting a before/after delta with real percentages — the strongest first-party quantitative evidence found for "reducing friction shortens time-to-first-success and raises completion." Source: https://www.twilio.com/en-us/blog/developers/redesigning-twilio-onboarding-experience-whats-new — **Confidence: MEDIUM** (primary company data, but no methodology, sample size, or independent verification disclosed).

- **GitHub's own engineering onboarding: 45 minutes → 5 minutes → 10 seconds via Codespaces prebuilds.** GitHub's internal bootstrap time for a new engineer to get a working environment on the github.com core repo (originally ~45 min including a 13GB clone) dropped to 5 minutes with early Codespaces optimization, then to **10 seconds** with "prebuilds" (pools of pre-cloned, pre-bootstrapped codespaces). Quoted from GitHub's own product manager Tanmayee Kamath: "Prebuilds were a huge part of how we meaningfully reduced the time-to-bootstrap in Codespaces for our core GitHub.com codebase." This is GitHub measuring itself, but it's a concrete, named, multi-year progression with real numbers. Sources: https://github.blog/engineering/infrastructure/githubs-engineering-team-moved-codespaces/, https://github.blog/news-insights/product-news/prebuilding-codespaces-is-generally-available/, https://www.itpro.com/software/development/363678/github-codespaces-drastically-reduces-developer-onboarding-times — **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH** (primary source is GitHub's own engineering blog; the 45-min→10-sec figures are internal, not third-party-audited, but the mechanism — clone+bootstrap time — is externally verifiable in principle).

- **"Time to first call" (TTFC) is treated by API-DX practitioners as the single most important onboarding metric, but the canonical citation (TechCrunch, 2021, "The most important API metric is time to first call," by Postman's Joyce Lin) contains almost no hard numbers** — it names Stripe, Algolia, and Twitter as companies whose interactive docs/public workspaces reduce TTFC, but gives no comparative time figures. Source: https://techcrunch.com/2021/07/12/the-most-important-api-metric-is-time-to-first-call/ — **Confidence: LOW-MEDIUM as a numeric source; MEDIUM as evidence the concept is industry-canonical** (this is an opinion/thought-leadership piece by a Postman DevRel exec, not a study).

- **Postman's own follow-up blog does have numbers, but they are anecdotal, non-randomized, and self-selected.** "Company A" went from 17 minutes to 10 minutes to first successful call (1.7x) after adding a Postman Collection; the post claims aggregate improvements "up to 56x faster" across unnamed publishers, sourced from ad hoc log-pulls or informal experiments run by the API publishers themselves, not from a controlled study. Source: https://blog.postman.com/improve-your-time-to-first-api-call-by-20x/ — **Confidence: LOW** (the author explicitly frames it as pulled logs / informal experiments, not a study; companies are anonymized so figures can't be checked).

- **Stripe is repeatedly cited as "the benchmark" for time-to-hello-world / TTFC** across multiple secondary sources (Fern, Mintlify, Moesif, Apidog blogs) — live runnable code samples in 7+ languages, test keys pre-filled into examples, and outcome-oriented docs ("accept your first payment" rather than "authenticate"). No secondary source could produce an actual TTFC number for Stripe; it's reputational/qualitative consensus, not measured. Sources: https://www.mintlify.com/blog/stripe-docs, https://www.moesif.com/blog/best-practices/api-product-management/the-stripe-developer-experience-and-docs-teardown/, https://apidog.com/blog/stripe-docs/ — **Confidence: LOW-MEDIUM** (strong directional consensus across many independent secondary sources, but zero primary numeric data located; Stripe has not published its own TTFC figures publicly as far as this research found).

- **Directional industry claim, no citation trail:** multiple SEO/content-marketing pages (Cycloid, Fullscale.io, The CTO's Edge) assert "industry average time-to-productivity is 2-4 weeks, automated onboarding compresses to under a day" — this figure could not be traced to any underlying primary study; treat as **unsourced marketing copy, Confidence: LOW**, included only to flag that this often-repeated number has no verifiable basis.

**Bottom line for item 1:** treat "time to hello world" as a real, industry-agreed concept with essentially no independently-verified benchmark data. The best available evidence is a handful of single-company before/after deltas (Twilio, GitHub) that are directionally consistent (fewer manual steps → faster first success → higher activation) but not generalizable studies.

## 2. CLI scaffolding wizards vs. static templates

**This is the best-evidenced item of the four — multiple tool-makers have published explicit, quotable rationale for choosing an interactive CLI wizard over a plain template/boilerplate repo. This is design reasoning, not measured data, but it's primary-source and consistent across independent teams.**

- **Create React App (Facebook, 2016) — original announcement explicitly diagnoses the template/boilerplate problem it was built to solve.** Direct quotes from the original post: "by choosing React (and inherently JSX), you've unwittingly opted into a confusing nest of build tools, boilerplate, linters, & time-sinks"; existing tools "are plugin platforms and don't directly acknowledge each other's existence. They leave it up to the users to wire them together"; "It is far too easy to get dragged into fighting small incompatibilities, unsatisfied peerDependencies, and illegible configuration files." The post explicitly invokes Kathy Sierra's cognitive-load argument for curation over choice: "If your UX asks the user to make choices, for example, even if those choices are both clear and useful, the act of deciding is a cognitive drain." CRA's design deliberately hid all config initially (zero decisions), with `eject` as an escape hatch, explicitly framed as "forking our configuration and going your own way." Source: https://legacy.reactjs.org/blog/2016/07/22/create-apps-with-no-configuration.html — **Confidence: HIGH** (primary source, the tool's own creators explaining their own design rationale).

- **create-next-app (Vercel, 2019 relaunch) — explicit design goals: speed and a guided interactive flow, quantified.** Vercel's own blog: rebuilt "to provide the best possible developer experience," headlining "Interactive Experience: Running npx create-next-app (with no arguments) now launches an interactive experience that guides you through setting up a project," plus a **quantified** dependency/init-speed improvement: init dropped to "as quick as one second," package size cut from 5.38 MB to 604 kB (a 4.7 MB / ~88% reduction), explicitly framed as improving first-run friction. It also supports pulling a template from the examples collection as an alternative to the wizard (`--example`), i.e., Vercel offers both paths but leads with the wizard as default. Source: https://nextjs.org/blog/create-next-app — **Confidence: HIGH** (primary source, official product blog with concrete before/after numbers on the tool itself, even though these are packaging-size metrics rather than user-study metrics).

- **Yeoman's own framing draws an explicit line between "simple boilerplate copiers" and "advanced generators [that] can use custom prompts."** Yeoman generically distinguishes plain copy-a-boilerplate generators from ones that interactively ask the user's preferences to "scaffold a tailor made project," and pitches composability (generators calling generators) as the advantage a static template repo cannot offer. This is stated as Yeoman's own value proposition rather than backed by usage data. Sources: https://yeoman.io/, https://itnext.io/generating-code-with-yeoman-js-f13e0da87374 — **Confidence: MEDIUM** (primary-adjacent — official site plus a practitioner writeup — but philosophy/marketing copy, not evidence of outcomes).

- **create-t3-app's stated philosophy is modularity via prompts, not maximal defaults** — its docs frame the interactive CLI as letting each stack piece be optional and generated per-answer, contrasted implicitly with a fixed template that bakes in one config. Source: https://create.t3.gg/ — **Confidence: LOW-MEDIUM** (documentation framing, not a study; found via secondary aggregation, not independently verified against primary docs text in full).

- **The scaffolder-vs-template split is not universal even within a single language ecosystem — some ecosystems standardize on interactive wizards (Rails' `rails new`, Phoenix's `mix phx.new`, Next.js/T3), others explicitly ship a bare project-skeleton generator and leave "framework-shaped" scaffolding to the community (Rust's `cargo new`, Go).** Nearly all such tools accept both prompt-mode and flag/non-interactive mode, i.e., the wizard-vs-template choice is usually offered as a spectrum (interactive by default, scriptable via flags) rather than an either/or. This synthesis point could not be traced to one canonical primary doc/RFC — no official Cargo or npm design document explaining the interactive-vs-flags tradeoff was located despite searching. **Confidence: LOW** on the "kit not framework" framing specifically (assembled from secondary discussion, not a primary RFC); note explicitly that a rigorous primary source for Cargo/npm's own reasoning was **not found**.

- **create-react-app's later deprecation (2023 React docs, officially archived ~2025) reinforces a cautionary counter-datapoint: a wizard/zero-config tool that stops evolving becomes a liability.** Multiple secondary sources note CRA's Webpack-based config went stale relative to Vite, its `eject` design meant customization required forking the entire config, and by the end CRA carried ~205MB of node_modules vs. Vite's ~34MB. This doesn't bear on wizard-vs-template per se, but is relevant evidence that **the wizard pattern's long-term risk is maintenance/staleness of the generated template it hands out, not the interactive-prompt UX itself.** Sources: https://github.com/reduxjs/redux-toolkit/issues/3384, multiple migration-guide blog posts (secondary, converging) — **Confidence: MEDIUM** (widely corroborated across many independent secondary sources, though no single rigorous primary post-mortem from the React/CRA team was found).

**Bottom line for item 2:** the strongest evidence is that **tool-makers who explicitly compared the two chose wizards for the same stated reason — reducing decision/cognitive load at first contact — and treated a plain template/boilerplate repo as the thing they were escaping**, not as a competitive alternative they benchmarked against. No source found actually A/B-tested wizard-vs-template completion or activation; this is design philosophy, consistently repeated, not measured outcome data.

## 3. In-browser code editor completion/abandonment data

**Weakest-evidenced item. No platform-specific (CodeSandbox/StackBlitz/Replit/Codespaces/Glitch) published completion or abandonment numbers were found despite extensive searching. The one rigorous data point located is a peer-reviewed academic paper on an unnamed/different browser-based coding tutorial platform, plus general MOOC completion-rate literature that is adjacent but not about in-browser IDEs specifically.**

- **"Predicting Abandonment in Online Coding Tutorials" (Mason, Dorn, et al. or similar authorship — IEEE VL/HCC 2017) is the one genuinely rigorous, peer-reviewed data point found.** Studied interaction logs from an online programming game/tutorial platform (browser-based coding environment) and built ML classifiers to predict abandonment. Key figures: classifiers correctly predicted **61–76% of learners who would not complete the next level**, across the first five levels, with an average AUC of 0.68; account activation and help-seeking behaviors were negatively correlated with abandonment (i.e., reduced it), while difficulty/disengagement signals predicted higher abandonment. This is the strongest primary academic evidence located for in-browser coding-tool drop-off, but it studies one unnamed platform, not CodeSandbox/StackBlitz/Replit by name. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.04291 (IEEE VL/HCC 2017) — **Confidence: HIGH** (peer-reviewed, published venue, concrete methodology and numbers) **but LOW relevance-specificity** — it is not about the named commercial in-browser editors this research was asked to cover.

- **General MOOC completion-rate research (adjacent, not in-browser-IDE-specific) is well-established and rigorous, and useful as a floor/ceiling reference:** Katy Jordan's widely-cited analysis of 221 MOOCs (multiple institutions/platforms) found a **median completion rate of 12.6%**, range 0.7%–52.1%; her earlier 29-MOOC study found a 6.8% average. Auto-graded MOOCs (7.7%) beat peer-assessed MOOCs (4.8%) on completion. Course length was the strongest negative predictor — shorter courses complete better. Sources: http://www.katyjordan.com/MOOCproject.html, https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3899451 — **Confidence: HIGH as MOOC data, MEDIUM relevance** (this is lecture-based MOOC completion, not specifically browser-IDE "type code, see it run" onboarding — but it's the most rigorous adjacent completion-rate literature available, and the "shorter = better completion" finding is plausibly transferable).

- **freeCodeCamp (project-based, in-browser coding curriculum) reports higher completion than typical MOOCs: ~22% for full certifications, ~45% for individual sections**, attributed to its project-based (vs. lecture-based) structure — cited as consistent with the general finding that "hands-on project" courses reach ~65% completion, roughly double lecture-only self-paced courses. **This figure could not be traced to an official freeCodeCamp primary publication** — it appears in aggregator/statistics sites (Skillademia) without a clear citation to freeCodeCamp's own data release. Source: https://www.skillademia.com/statistics/online-course-completion-statistics/ — **Confidence: LOW** (numbers exist and are specific, but the primary source could not be verified — flagging explicitly that this is an unverified secondary claim, not confirmed against freeCodeCamp's own reporting).

- **No completion/abandonment/drop-off numbers were found for CodeSandbox, StackBlitz, Replit, Glitch, or GitHub Codespaces onboarding specifically**, despite multiple targeted searches. These companies do not appear to have published funnel data (signup → first running sandbox, or similar) publicly. This is an explicit gap: **a rigorous number does not exist in public sources for the named in-browser editor tools** — any claim about their completion rates would be speculation.

- **Codecademy: no completion-rate statistics located at all**, primary or secondary, beyond the platform's own acknowledgment (via its community forum) that it "tracks course completion rate as a KPI" without publishing the number. **Confidence: N/A — explicitly no data found.**

**Bottom line for item 3:** the honest finding is an evidence gap. The one peer-reviewed number (61-76% predictable abandonment, VL/HCC 2017) is rigorous but about an unnamed platform. The MOOC literature (12.6% median completion, Katy Jordan) is rigorous but about lecture MOOCs, not code-run-in-browser tools. Platform-specific numbers for the actual commercial in-browser editors this research was asked about (CodeSandbox, StackBlitz, Replit, Codespaces, Glitch) do not appear to exist in any public, citable form.

## 4. GitHub / Stripe / Vercel DX research + academic HCI papers

- **GitHub Octoverse 2021 — documentation and friction-reduction productivity claims, GitHub's own survey data (40,000+ developers, telemetry from 4M+ repos).** Quoted claim: "Sharing information through READMEs, contribution guidelines, and issues... make developers 55% more productive." Also: "developers' performance at work can increase by up to 87% when reusing others' code is easy," and automation reduces friction enough to lift performance "27% in open source and 43% at work." Source: https://github.blog/news-insights/octoverse/the-2021-state-of-the-octoverse/ (full PDF: https://octoverse.github.com/2021/static/octoverse-report-2021.pdf) — **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH** (primary source, large stated sample, but methodology for converting survey responses into "% more productive" figures is not fully transparent in secondary summaries — the underlying survey instrument wasn't independently verified here).

- **GitHub Octoverse (2025/2026 edition) — "nearly 80% of new developers on GitHub use GitHub Copilot within their first week."** Cited as evidence that AI-assisted tooling is now a default expectation in first-week onboarding, part of GitHub's broader "convenience loop" framing (early positive AI-tool experience shapes subsequent language/tool choices). Source: https://github.blog/news-insights/octoverse/octoverse-a-new-developer-joins-github-every-second-as-ai-leads-typescript-to-1/ — **Confidence: HIGH** (primary source, GitHub's own platform telemetry, not a survey — this is closer to hard usage data than the 2021 productivity percentages).

- **Stripe: no primary Stripe-published research/data on TTFC or onboarding metrics was located.** Everything found about "Stripe's DX" is third-party reputational analysis (Mintlify, Moesif, Apidog blog teardowns) praising interactive/runnable docs, pre-filled test keys, and outcome-first framing, with zero numbers sourced to Stripe itself. Stripe's own publication *Increment* (founded by Stripe's Susan Fowler) covers API design broadly via industry guest contributors (Adobe, Airbnb, Kong, PubNub) rather than publishing Stripe's own onboarding metrics. Source: https://increment.com/apis/, https://stripe.com/blog/increment — **Confidence: explicitly flagging absence** — Stripe is the most-cited DX benchmark in this entire research pass, and yet **no primary, numeric Stripe source was found**. Treat every "Stripe is the gold standard" claim in this document (and likely in the broader field) as reputational consensus, not measured data, unless a primary source surfaces later.

- **Vercel: no dedicated DX blog post was found specifically explaining the create-next-app wizard's design tradeoffs beyond the product-announcement post already cited in item 2.** Vercel's current blog content skews toward v0 (AI UI generation) rather than classic scaffolding-tool DX writing. **Confidence: explicitly flagging absence** — treat item 2's create-next-app citation as the extent of primary Vercel material found; no separate "why we chose a wizard over templates" essay exists at time of research.

- **Academic HCI: CHI 2026 papers exist on adjacent onboarding-friction topics but not on "fork template vs. CLI wizard vs. browser IDE" directly.** Two CHI 2026 papers surfaced: (1) "Starting From Scratch Again and Again" (Northwestern, Tran/Fasiang/Kanwal/O'Rourke) — studies why high schoolers form negative perceptions of **block-based** programming environments, tracing friction to how novice tools represent code and scaffold learning; relevant as a data point that novice-facing tool *representation* choices measurably shape sentiment, but it's about block-based visual programming for K-12, not CLI/repo/browser onboarding for working developers. DOI: https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772318.3791825 — **Confidence: MEDIUM relevance, HIGH rigor** (peer-reviewed, but domain is K-12 block-based programming, a reach vs. this doc's target audience). (2) "I Just Need GPT to Refine My Prompts" (CHI 2026) — studies onboarding/help-seeking friction in **generative 3D modeling** tools, finding that lowering entry barriers can introduce new friction (unfamiliar affordances, opaque errors, invisible troubleshooting labor) — directionally useful as a caution that "easier first step" isn't automatically "less total friction," but again not about code scaffolding specifically. DOI: https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772318.3791124 — **Confidence: MEDIUM relevance, HIGH rigor**.
  - **Explicit gap:** no CHI/UIST/ICSE/VL-HCC paper was found that directly compares fork-template vs. CLI-wizard vs. browser-IDE onboarding for developer tools. This is a real evidence gap in the academic literature, not just a search miss — the two topically-closest CHI 2026 papers had to be reached via adjacent domains (block-based ed-tech, generative 3D tools).

**Bottom line for item 4:** GitHub is the only one of the three companies named with genuine, citable primary DX data (Octoverse, both the 2021 productivity-correlation numbers and the 2025/2026 Copilot first-week adoption number). Stripe's DX reputation is essentially unsupported by any located primary Stripe source — it's pure secondary consensus. Vercel's only primary artifact is the product announcement already used in item 2. Academic HCI coverage of this exact question (scaffolding pattern choice for developer onboarding) does not appear to exist; the closest CHI 2026 work is in adjacent domains (K-12 block programming, generative 3D tools).
